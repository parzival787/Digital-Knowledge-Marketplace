Abstract—Knowledge-sharing platforms must support docu-
ment discovery, verifiable asset registration, and controlled access
to stored content. This paper presents DKM, a hybrid prototype
that combines ERC-721 asset registration on the Polygon Amoy
testnet with encrypted off-chain storage and semantic retrieval.
Uploaded documents receive LLM-generated metadata; nor-
malized MiniLM summary embeddings support vector search,
and metadata relationships supply a supplementary graph-based
ranking signal. Content is protected with AES-GCM envelope
encryption, and the backend, which holds the master key, verifies
wallet-linked entitlement before returning decrypted files. A post-
quantum key-release layer is designed but not implemented,
and no marketplace purchase-to-decryption result is reported.
We additionally evaluate a four-qubit variational quantum fea-
ture model, executed by classical statevector simulation, on a
controlled corpus of 96 authored documents and 32 queries
(16 validation, 16 test). On the test split, original MiniLM
embeddings achieved NDCG@10 of 0.9053, against 0.7027 for
a four-component PCA baseline with ridge expansion and 0.6093
for the trained quantum model; the quantum representation
did not improve retrieval. The study offers a prototype and a
bounded, single-seed assessment, and identifies centralized key
custody, a small author-built corpus, in-domain queries, and
missing keyword and ablation baselines as limitations.

I. INTRODUCTION
A. Problem Context and Existing Marketplaces
Knowledge assets — research documents, datasets, trained
models, and curated domain resources — are increasingly
traded as reusable digital resources, yet their exchange depends
heavily on centralized platforms that mediate publication,
discovery, transactions, and access. Such platforms concentrate
metadata, ownership records, and access policies under one
operator, which limits independent verification and creates
a single point of failure. AQUASTREAM identifies opaque
pricing, privacy risk, single points of failure, and limited
verifiable ownership as core limitations of centralized data
marketplaces [3]. Decentralization addresses the transaction
and ownership layer; it does not by itself help buyers discover
or assess heterogeneous assets by semantic relevance and
quality.
Existing decentralized marketplaces use smart contracts and
decentralized storage for verifiable ownership, programmable
transactions, and controlled access [3], [4], [7]. AQUAS-
TREAM, the closest foundation for DKM, combines ERC-
721 ownership, IPFS storage through Pinata, AES-256/RSA
encryption, and ERC-20 payments [3]. Across the 18 papers
reviewed in Section II, we found four recurring limitations.
(i) Semantic discovery is absent: PACDAM ranks candi-
dates with rule-based weighted scoring [5], and the other
marketplaces provide no semantic search. (ii) No automated
quality assessment exists; MetaTrade offers only manual buyer
ratings [7]. (iii) Duplicate detection appears only as perceptual-
hash plagiarism checks for AI-generated content [7], not for
documents or datasets. (iv) Post-quantum protection is not
integrated into marketplace access control, although post-
quantum blockchain work exists separately [14]–[16]. These
statements are scoped to the reviewed set and are not claims
about all existing work.
B. Approach, Research Questions, and Contributions
DKM retains AQUASTREAM’s separation of on-chain as-
set state from off-chain content and adds knowledge-specific
metadata, retrieval, and access mediation. We do not claim
novelty for NFTs, AES, BLAKE3, IPFS, MiniLM, VQC,
or QAOA. The system generates LLM metadata at upload,
embeds summaries with MiniLM, applies a graph-propagation
and QUBO heuristic, rejects byte-identical duplicates, and
encrypts protected content. Post-quantum key release (Lit
Protocol, ML-KEM-768) is a planned extension that is neither
implemented nor evaluated here.

II. LITERATUREREVIEW
A. Decentralized marketplaces.AQUASTREAM [3] trades
datasets as ERC-721 tokens with IPFS storage and AES-
256/RSA key release. Cai et al. [1] monetize aggregated
truth values mined from crowdsensed streams rather than
documents, and Bernab´e-Rodr´ıguez et al. [2] coordinate secure
multi-party computation through a blockchain. MetaAICM [4]
and MetaTrade [7] trade machine-learning data and models
and AI-generated content in the metaverse; MetaTrade adds
hash-gated key release, perceptual-hash plagiarism checks,
and inheritable royalties. PACDAM [5] targets cross-chain
asset exchange, CloudChain [6] AI compute, and Aljohani et
al. [8] reviewer anonymity. These works contribute ownership,
payment, privacy, or access mechanics; none treats semantic
discovery or knowledge-specific quality as first-class.
B. Semantic discovery.Zhu et al. [18] describe the query-
rewriting, retrieval, reranking, and reading pipeline, note that
term-based retrieval suffers vocabulary mismatch, that dense
retrievers can generalize poorly out of domain, and that diver-
sity is a classical reranking objective. Wang et al. [17] model
relationships among knowledge-graph triples on a blockchain
for quality consensus, not for discovering assets. No reviewed
paper covers graph-based asset discovery, combines semantic
discovery with a marketplace or blockchain context, or tests
variational quantum embeddings for retrieval; the VQC exper-
iment here is therefore exploratory within that scope.
C. Storage and integrity.Khalid et al. [9] survey Storj,
Filecoin, and Sia and note that content identifiers are public:
confidentiality requires client-side encryption before storage,
while the identifier gives integrity only. The survey’s attack
taxonomy is a useful threat checklist; DKM has no proof-of-
storage mechanism.
D. Encryption and access control.Bose [10] combines
threshold encryption with universal zero-knowledge proofs,
and ZK-PEAC [11] embeds blinded access policies with time-
bound tokens and game-based proofs for health records. Abla
et al. [12] (FHE-based) and Xue et al. [13] (fine-grained, ZKP-
based) give fair, privacy-preserving data trading. These offer
stronger guarantees than DKM’s backend-mediated, coarse
per-asset entitlement check, and DKM implements none of
them.

III. SYSTEMARCHITECTURE ANDIMPLEMENTATION
A. System Overview and Architecture
This is a hybrid architecture with decentralized components;
backend, database, key custody, and RPC services remain trust
dependencies. A user connects a wallet through a Next.js
frontend. A FastAPI backend coordinates an Ollama-served
Llama 3.2 model for metadata, MiniLM for 384-dimensional
embeddings, graph propagation with a classical QUBO selec-
tion heuristic, AES-GCM encryption with BLAKE3 integrity
checking, and Web3.py for access verification. PostgreSQL
with pgvector, IPFS through Pinata, and the KnowledgeAsset
contract on Polygon Amoy form the storage layer (Fig. 1).
Data placement is as follows. The blockchain holds as-
set registration, creator-linked state, references, and access
state. IPFS holds ciphertext for assets uploaded through the
protected path; published metadata may remain public. Post-
greSQL holds accounts, sessions, searchable metadata, nor-
malized vectors, wrapped content keys, and application link-
age. The content master key resides in backend configuration.
Wallet signing, database persistence, and chain confirmation
are separate operations, not an atomic transaction. Fig. 1 is a
design figure and does not show that every route operates end
to end; Table II records actual status.

E. Quality Assessment and Fraud/Duplicate Detection
The LLM quality score is uncalibrated and has not been
validated against independent human judgment. The BLAKE3
duplicate guard detects byte-identical files only, not para-
phrases, plagiarism, or semantic near-duplicates. Hash equality
can reject identical byte sequences but cannot establish orig-
inality, truth, or authorship, and the “fraud detector” checks
duplicates and very small files rather than acting as a learned
classifier. No human ratings, score correlations, or duplicate
precision, recall, or F1 exist for this system.
F . Storage and Content Integrity
Files and metadata are pinned to IPFS through Pinata, and
PostgreSQL links the resulting CIDs to the vector entry and
the Polygon token. Content addressing via IPFS CID provides
integrity verification, not confidentiality — the encryption
layer is the sole confidentiality mechanism. BLAKE3 supports
integrity checking but is not encryption. Pinning availability,
integrity, and confidentiality are different properties. Assets
uploaded through the protected path are pinned as AES-GCM
ciphertext, whereas published metadata may remain public and
legacy public-file assets are not retroactively protected.

G. Security Layer and Post-Quantum Cryptography
Lit Protocol token-gated access and CRYSTALS-Kyber key
encapsulation are designed for Phase 4 and have not yet been
benchmarked. The currently deployed system uses AES-GCM
envelope encryption with backend-held master key custody.
Each protected file is encrypted under a random 256-bit
key with AES-GCM, which provides authenticated encryption,
and the file key is wrapped under the backend master key;
wrapped keys are stored in PostgreSQL. The backend sees
uploaded plaintext and can decrypt any stored file, so this is not
end-to-end encryption against the operator. Before returning
content, the backend authenticates the session, verifies wallet
control through an expiring challenge, and checks entitlement
against the contract. BLAKE3 is an integrity hash, not a key-
establishment protocol. Lit Protocol and ML-KEM-768 remain
a planned, unbenchmarked extension; no latency or post-
quantum security result is reported, and the deployed system
has no demonstrated post-quantum protection. Table IV lists
controls evidenced by the implementation and their residual
limits; it is not a formal proof, audit, or penetration test.
H. Frontend and Wallet Integration
The frontend is a Next.js application built with React and
TypeScript that uses wagmi, RainbowKit (not ConnectKit),
and MetaMask to connect the wallet, request transaction
signatures, and call backend APIs. A connected wallet shows
only which address the user controls; entitlement to a stored
document is established by the backend check of Section III-G.
IV. EXPERIMENTALMETHODOLOGY ANDRESULTS
A. Methodology
The corpus has 96 documents authored by the researchers;
all 96 form the retrieval corpus, and 32 document variants are
excluded from model fitting. There are 32 authored queries, 16
validation and 16 test, with disjoint topics. Relevance labels
are authored topic/domain rules on a 3/1/0 scale, not indepen-
dent human judgments. Query topics also occur in training
documents, so this is in-domain retrieval, and query wording
overlaps document subjects, so scores can overestimate real-
istic quality. Training fits PCA and scaling on 64 documents;
expansion is trained on document vectors only; no test query,
label, or metric enters training or parameter selection. Four
representations are compared on identical documents, queries,
and labels (Table V).[VERIFY: domain mix and number
of domains were not recorded in the retained report; add
if available]
B. VQC Retrieval Results (RQ1, RQ2)
The central result is negative. The trained VQC obtained test
NDCG@10 of 0.6093, below both original MiniLM (0.9053)
and PCA4 with ridge expansion (0.7027). Training raised test
NDCG@10 from 0.6050 to 0.6093 relative to the untrained
circuit, a difference of 0.0043 that cannot be separated from
noise with 16 queries and one seed. Training loss fell from
0.4852 to 0.0987, yet retrieval did not improve beyond the
classical compressed baseline, so optimizing Gram alignment

no scalable oracle, hardware speedup, or quantum advantage
is claimed.

V. LIMITATIONS ANDDISCUSSION
The evidence is narrow. The 96-document corpus was
authored by the researchers with rule-based labels and over-

=== PAGE 7 ===
lapping query wording; results come from one seed with no
confidence intervals; 16 test queries give coarse estimates;
retrieval is in-domain; and the VQC ran as noiseless statevector
simulation. The negative result applies to this circuit, objective,
and corpus, not to quantum methods in general. On the system
side, the backend master key is a centralized trust dependency,
purchase-to-decryption and Marketplace listing are unverified,
post-quantum key release is planned, and testnet gas and
latency do not transfer to mainnet. Blockchain registration
does not establish legal authorship or copyright.
VI. CONCLUSION
DKM is a hybrid prototype that extends an
AQUASTREAM-style ERC-721 marketplace with LLM
metadata, MiniLM semantic retrieval, a supplementary asset
graph, and AES-GCM encrypted off-chain storage under
backend-held key custody. Across the 18 papers reviewed, we
found no system combining semantic discovery, automated
quality assessment, and post-quantum access control for
knowledge assets. On a controlled corpus of 96 authored
documents, original MiniLM reached test NDCG@10 of
0.9053, while the trained four-qubit VQC reached 0.6093,
below a four-component PCA baseline (0.7027); this negative
result carries no claim of quantum advantage, and the optional
QAOA simulation likewise favored classical optimization.
Future work is to validate the purchase-to-decryption flow,
implement and benchmark post-quantum key release, and
run ablations of graph and QUBO reranking against BM25,
personalized PageRank, and Maximal Marginal Relevance
with repeated seeds and independent corpora.
